The story the curves tell

This chart captures something remarkable: despite decades of regulation changes designed to slow cars down, F1 keeps getting faster. Every time the FIA takes away speed, engineers find it somewhere else.

Monza tells the clearest story — from ~1:35 in the early 70s (after chicanes were added to tame 200mph slipstreaming battles) to 1:18.8 in 2025. That's 17 seconds faster around the same circuit. At Monza's average speed of 264 km/h, those 17 seconds represent a gap of about 1.2 kilometers — nearly a quarter of the lap.

Spa-Francorchamps shows the most dramatic single drop — the old 14km circuit was replaced by the current 7km layout in 1979, which is why the line appears to jump. But even on the shorter track, times have dropped from ~2:07 to ~1:43 over four decades.

Monaco is the most impressive in some ways — gaining time on streets lined with barriers, where every tenth of a second means getting closer to the wall. The drop from ~1:27 in the early 70s to ~1:10 today shows how much driver confidence has grown alongside car capability.

The visible "bumps" in the curves correspond to regulation resets: grooved tyres in 1998 (times got slower), V6 turbo-hybrids in 2014 (brief slowdown), and ground effect in 2022 (another reset). Each time, within 2–3 years, the cars were faster than ever. The engineering ingenuity of F1 teams is the constant thread: give them a rulebook, and they'll find the loophole that makes the car faster.
